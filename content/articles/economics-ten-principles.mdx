> 材料来自 Dr. Azevedo（University of Central Missouri 经济系）一门经济学导论课的第 2 章。这是学习笔记，讲稿已按我的理解重排，不是逐字稿。

我决定认真补一遍经济学，起因很具体：我发现自己在讨论投资的时候，经常把几件不同的事混为一谈——把「已经亏了多少」当成继续持有的理由，把「这笔钱没花出去」当成零成本，把「利好政策」当成市场一定会买账。

这些不是知识缺口，是**概念用错了**。经济学导论课的第一章基本上就在修这个。它讲的东西不新鲜，但是把「成本」这个词的定义改一下，很多纠结会自动消失。

下面是我整理下来的十条原理，按对我有没有用来排序，不是按课程顺序。

## 先纠正一个误解：经济学不是研究钱

开课第一天，老师发了一份问卷，列出一堆话题让学生勾「这是不是经济学」。

商业周期、失业率、通货膨胀、银行系统、股价变动、利润最大化——大家全勾了。但房主多久割一次草坪、人在高速上开多快、学生在讲座上能专注多久、跑垒员什么时候决定盗垒、鱼为什么成群游动——几乎没人勾。

答案是：**两组全都算经济学**。

经济学在最底层研究的是「人在稀缺条件下怎么做决策」。凡是有人在做选择的地方，都是它的地盘。人们只对跟美元、股价、银行沾边的东西举手，恰恰说明误解了这门学科。

甚至不限于人。有文献研究笼子里的老鼠：按一下杠杆掉一块食物，把「价格」提高（改成多按几次才掉一块），老鼠就少吃。**价格机制对老鼠同样有效。**

这个定义一换，经济学立刻从「商科专业课」变成「一套看世界的通用框架」。

## 一、机会成本：成本不是你花掉的钱，是你放弃的那个次优选项

这是十条里我认为最值得记的一条。

日常语境里，成本 = 花掉的美元。一杯咖啡 2 美元，成本是 2 美元。经济学把它换成了：成本 = **你为了得到它而放弃的一切**。

课上有两个例子，都比定义本身好记。

**去上课的成本。** 上课本身不发生金钱交易（学费已经付过了）。你放弃的是那段时间里**本来会做的事**——如果不上课你会睡觉，成本就是睡觉；你会打游戏，成本就是打游戏；父母正好来城里要带你吃饭而你因为上课去不了，成本就是那顿饭。

注意这里的关键词：**不是「多少分钟」，而是「那些分钟里你放弃的那件事」。**

**买披萨的成本。** 披萨 10 美元。机会成本不是那 10 美元本身，而是**这 10 美元本可以买到的其他东西**，加上打电话、起身开门的那点力气。

有个学生常犯的错值得单独拎出来：有人觉得「不上课的话我能看电视、打游戏、散步……有无限多种可能，所以成本是无限的」。错。机会成本**永远只算次优的那一个**，不是所有放弃项之和。

推论就是那句被说烂了的话：天下没有免费的午餐。按「花多少美元」定义成本，有些东西看起来免费；按机会成本定义，一切都伴随代价。

**为什么这条对投资者最要命**：因为散户嘴里说的「成本」，十有八九是会计成本或沉没成本，不是机会成本。

- 「我已经被套 30%，现在卖就是实亏」——沉没成本不是成本。持有这只股票的真实成本，是**这笔钱投到次优选项（比如宽基指数）本可以赚到的收益**。
- 「我没炒股，钱放活期，零成本」——成本是你放弃的国债或货基收益。
- 「我就随便看看盘，不花什么」——每天两小时，次优选项可能是学一门能涨价的技能。

把成本的定义换过来，「要不要卖」这个问题会从情绪题变成算术题。

## 二、边际思维：只比较多做一点的收益和成本

理性人不是在「做不做」之间选，是在「再多一点」之间选。

**边际变动** = 对现行计划的小幅增量调整。**边际收益 MB** = 再多做一个单位带来的收益变化量；**边际成本 MC** = 再多做一个单位带来的成本变化量。决策规则只有一句：

> 做，当且仅当 MB > MC。

课上的例子是学习。你不会坐下来宣布「我要学 82 分 17 秒」，你是先学起来，然后随着激励变化动态调整：朋友都在忙、学的又正好是全新突破，MB 高 MC 低，继续；喜欢的朋友敲门叫你出去，MC 上升了——只要它还小于 MB，你还是继续学；一旦 MC 超过 MB，你合上书走人。

反过来的极端情况更说明问题：就算你正处在「脑子里的乌云散开」的顿悟状态（MB 极高），如果有人拿枪指着你让你停（MC 变成死亡），你照样停。

**所以重要的从来不是 MB 或 MC 各自有多大，而是两者的相对大小。**

对投资：加仓减仓问的是边际，不是总量——不是「这只票便宜不便宜」，而是「用**下一笔**钱买它，比买次优选项好多少」。止损同理：亏损扩大时，继续持有的 MB 在降、MC 在升，交叉点就是该动的时候，而不是「等回本」。

## 三、激励：价格变了，人就会换

所有行为都是对激励的回应。这句话听起来像废话，但它是十条里最容易被漏掉的一条。

激励分三类，可见度递减：

- **经济激励**：美元、分数。最容易看见，也最容易理解。
- **社会激励**：渴望被接纳、害怕被嘲笑。难看见，但驱动了我们更多的行为。
- **道德激励**：对是非对错的判断。最难观察。

而且**同一激励，不同人的反应不同**——给全班同样的加分规则，有人拼命学，有人无动于衷。

课上最精彩的一段是用它来拆解「石油还能用多少年」。

常规算法是：全球已探明储量 ÷ 年消耗量 = 还剩多少年。老师给的数字算出来是「三十多年」，然后他说：**这个算法是错的，正确答案是永远不会用完。**

他的类比是一个装满 5310 亿颗花生的房间，规定壳不能带出去。第一天，随手就能摸到花生，成本接近零；一个月后，花生壳堆了一层，你得扒开壳才能挖到好花生，**吃花生的成本开始上升**；成本持续上升，你吃得越来越少；到最后，壳堆到十英尺厚，扒开它比「开车去 Walmart 花两块五买一袋」更贵——你主动转向替代品。

**你永远不会吃掉房间里最后一颗花生。** 石油同理。不是靠教育，也不是靠环保意识，而是替代品变便宜了，人就换了。

我自己补两点课上没讲的：

一是这个论证区分的是**经济枯竭**（开采成本高于替代品）与**物理枯竭**（分子层面没有了）。经济学关心前者，结论成立。

二是它**完全没考虑外部性**——碳排放的气候成本不由消费者承担。所以「不会用完」和「应该继续用完」是两回事，这正好是第七条原理要补的。

顺带说一句：课上那组石油数字是**旧口径**。按 BP《世界能源统计年鉴》近年数据，全球已探明储量约 1.7–1.77 万亿桶，年消耗约 365 亿桶，静态储采比约 47 年。数字要更新，但结论不受影响——因为这套论证本身就是在否定「储量÷消耗」这个算法。

## 四、权衡取舍：稀缺性决定了没有免费午餐

个人层面很好理解：收入花在披萨上，就不能花在课本上；时间用来复习，就不能睡觉。

社会层面有两大权衡：

- **大炮与黄油**：国防 vs 消费品与服务。投入国防的每一份资源，都拿不出修路、发奖学金。
- **效率与平等**：效率是蛋糕有多大，平等是蛋糕分得均不均。更强调平等，就要从能者手中转移资源，蛋糕本身会变小。

老师特别强调了一句：说存在这个权衡，**不等于主张什么都不做**。每个经济体都在做再分配，只是要认清「做得越多，可供分配的总量越少」。

## 五、贸易使人人更好：这是正和，不是零和

扑克是零和——我多赢的每一分都是别人亏的。自愿贸易不是，它是**正和**，双方都能获益。

反证很利落：如果你主张少做交易，那为什么不只买本国货？如果只买本国货是好的，为什么不只买本州货、本市货？交易圈越小，你得自己生产的东西就越多，留给其他事的时间就越少。

注意措辞：原理说的是「**能**让每个人更好」，不是「**总会**让每个人更好」。现实里确有受损者，这是贸易政治经济学的核心议题。

## 六、市场，以及它的边界

第六条：市场是组织经济活动的好方式。第七条：政府有时可以改善市场结果。这两条必须成对读。

课上特地澄清了一个高频误读：**经济学家说的「自由市场」，不是卖家可以为所欲为、不是零监管**——而是卖家和买家都在法律允许的范围内自由。不能对顾客谎报产品质量，不能购买非法物品。

「自由市场 = 没有监管」是彻底的误读。

对照面是计划经济，特征是生产资料归政府所有、由政府决定生产什么。第六条主张市场能胜出，但第七条立刻补上：**当出现市场失灵时**，政府干预有可能改善结果。典型的市场失灵是**外部性**——一个人的行为给其他人带来了成本，而市场价格没有反映它。

这里有个细节值得指出：Mankiw 原版的表述是「市场**通常**是组织经济活动的好方式」，课上讲成了「最佳方式」。这个 "usually" 不能丢——丢掉它，第七条就没有立锥之地了。

## 七、剩下三条，一句话交代

- **生活水平取决于生产能力**：一国的生活水平取决于它生产「别人想买的东西」的能力。对个人同样成立——你未来的生活水平，取决于你生产的、别人愿意付费的东西。这就是为什么「拥有别人愿意为之付费的技能」很重要，而不是「付出多少小时」很重要。
- **政府印太多钱，物价上涨**：通胀在根子上是货币现象。理解这一条，就理解为什么持有现金在通胀期是确定的购买力损失（这正是机会成本）。
- **短期内通胀与失业存在权衡**：降一个会推高另一个。关键限定词是「短期」——长期的菲利普斯曲线是垂直的，不存在稳定的替代关系。

## 我自己的清单

把十条压成五个问题，每次做决策前过一遍：

1. **我放弃的次优选项是什么？**（不是花多少钱，不是账面上亏多少）
2. **再多一点的收益，大于再多一点的成本吗？**（边际判断，不是总量判断）
3. **激励结构变了吗？谁的行为会因此改变？**
4. **这个价格里，有没有没被算进去的外部成本？**（会不会有政策来补）
5. **这是零和还是正和？**有没有让双方都更好的方案？

以及五个要避开的坑：把沉没成本当成本；把机会成本算成所有放弃项之和；只看绝对量不看相对量；用静态除法做资源或需求预测；把「自由市场」理解成无监管，或者反过来把「承认市场失灵」当成支持计划经济。

最后补一句语境提示：讲者的制度背景是美国，「自由市场 vs 计划经济」这个二分法对中国现实的解释力有限。中国是混合经济加强产业政策，分析时更该问的是：**这次政府是在补外部性，还是在替市场做选择。**

> Notes from Chapter 2 of an introductory economics course by Dr. Azevedo (University of Central Missouri). Reorganized around what I found useful — not a transcript.

I decided to properly study economics for a fairly specific reason: I kept noticing that when discussing investing, I was conflating several different things — treating "how much I'm already down" as a reason to keep holding, treating "money I didn't spend" as zero cost, treating "favorable policy" as a guarantee that the market will reward it.

That isn't a knowledge gap. It's **using the wrong concept**. Chapter 2 of any intro econ course is mostly about fixing exactly that. None of it is new information, but change the definition of one word — *cost* — and a surprising number of dilemmas dissolve on their own.

Below are the ten principles, ordered by usefulness to me rather than by lecture sequence.

## First, a correction: economics is not the study of money

On day one the instructor handed out a survey: check every topic you think economics can help you understand.

Business cycles, unemployment, inflation, the banking system, stock prices, profit maximization — everyone checked those. But how often a homeowner mows the lawn, how fast people drive on the highway, how long a student stays focused in a lecture, when a base runner decides to steal, why fish swim in schools — almost nobody checked those.

The answer: **both columns are economics.**

At its foundation, economics studies how people make decisions under scarcity. Anywhere someone is choosing, it applies. The fact that people only raised their hands for the money-adjacent items is precisely the misconception.

It doesn't even stop at humans. There is a literature on caged rats: press a lever, get a food pellet. Raise the "price" — make it take several presses per pellet — and the rat eats less. **Price mechanisms work on rats too.**

Change the definition and economics stops being a business-school subject and becomes a general framework for looking at the world.

## 1. Opportunity cost: cost is not what you spend, it's the next-best thing you gave up

This is the one I'd most want to remember.

In everyday usage, cost means dollars spent. A coffee costs two dollars. Economics replaces this with: cost is **everything you gave up to get it**.

Two examples from the lecture beat the definition itself.

**The cost of attending class.** No money changes hands at the door (tuition is already paid). What you give up is **what you would otherwise have been doing** — if you'd be sleeping, the cost is sleep; if you'd be gaming, the cost is gaming; if your parents are in town and wanted to take you to lunch, the cost is that lunch.

The key phrase: **not "how many minutes," but "what you would have done during those minutes."**

**The cost of a pizza.** Ten dollars. The opportunity cost isn't the ten dollars — it's **the other goods and services that ten dollars could have bought**, plus the small effort of calling and getting off the couch.

One student mistake is worth isolating: "If I skip class I could watch TV, play games, go for a walk — infinitely many things, so the cost is infinite." Wrong. Opportunity cost is **always just the single next-best option**, never the sum of everything forgone.

Hence the worn-out saying: there's no such thing as a free lunch. Define cost as dollars and some things look free. Define it as opportunity cost and everything carries a price.

**Why this matters most for investors**: the "cost" investors cite is almost always accounting cost or sunk cost, not opportunity cost.

- "I'm down 30%, selling now makes the loss real." Sunk cost is not a cost. The real cost of holding is **what that money would have earned in the next-best option**.
- "I'm not investing, it's sitting in a checking account, zero cost." The cost is the yield you gave up.
- "I'm just watching the tape." Two hours a day, whose next-best use might be learning a skill that actually raises your earning power.

Swap the definition and "should I sell" stops being an emotional question and becomes arithmetic.

## 2. Thinking at the margin: compare the next unit's benefit against its cost

Rational people don't choose between doing and not doing. They choose between a little more and a little less.

A **marginal change** is a small incremental adjustment to an existing plan. **Marginal benefit (MB)** is the change in benefit from one more unit; **marginal cost (MC)** is the change in cost from one more unit. The rule is one line:

> Act if and only if MB > MC.

The lecture's example is studying. You never sit down and announce "I will study for 82 minutes and 17 seconds." You start, then adjust as incentives shift: friends are busy and the material is finally clicking, so MB is high and MC low — keep going. A friend you like knocks and asks you out — MC rises. As long as it stays below MB, you keep studying; once it exceeds MB, you close the book.

The extreme case proves the point better: even in a state of genuine breakthrough (tremendous MB), if someone walks in with a gun and tells you to stop (MC becomes death), you stop.

**What matters is never how big MB or MC is on its own — only their size relative to each other.**

For investing: adding or trimming a position is a marginal question, not a total one — not "is this cheap," but "does the *next* dollar do better here than in the next-best option." Same with stops: as a loss widens, the MB of holding falls and the MC rises. The crossover is when you act — not when you get back to breakeven.

## 3. Incentives: change the price and people switch

All behavior is a response to incentives. It sounds trivially true, which is exactly why it's the principle most often forgotten.

Three kinds, in decreasing visibility:

- **Economic**: dollars, grades. Easiest to see.
- **Social**: wanting to be accepted, avoiding ridicule. Harder to see, and drives more of our behavior.
- **Moral**: your own sense of right and wrong. Hardest of all to observe.

And **the same incentive produces different responses in different people** — offer the whole class identical bonus points and some grind while others don't move.

The best passage in the lecture uses this to dismantle "how many years of oil are left."

The standard method: proven reserves divided by annual consumption. The numbers given produce "a bit over thirty years." Then: **that calculation is wrong, and the correct answer is never.**

The analogy is a room filled with 531 billion peanuts, with one rule — shells can't leave the room. Day one: peanuts everywhere, cost near zero. After a month: the shells you've been piling in the corner have slid over the good peanuts, and **the cost of eating a peanut starts rising**. It keeps rising, so you eat fewer. Eventually the shell layer is ten feet deep, and digging through it costs more than driving to Walmart and buying a bag for $2.50 — so you switch to the substitute.

**You will never eat the last good peanut.** Same with oil. Not because of education, not because of environmental awareness — because the substitute got cheaper.

Two things the lecture didn't say, which I'd add:

First, this argument distinguishes **economic depletion** (extraction cost exceeds the substitute) from **physical depletion** (the molecules are gone). Economics cares about the former, and the conclusion holds.

Second, it **ignores externalities entirely** — the climate cost of carbon isn't borne by the consumer. "We won't run out" and "we should keep burning it" are different claims. That's exactly what principle seven is for.

One correction: the lecture's oil figures are **an old vintage**. On recent BP *Statistical Review* numbers, proven reserves are roughly 1.7–1.77 trillion barrels against annual consumption near 36.5 billion, giving a static reserve-to-production ratio of about 47 years. Update the numbers; the conclusion is untouched — the whole point is that this division is the wrong operation.

## 4. Trade-offs: scarcity means no free lunch

Individually it's obvious: money on pizza isn't money on textbooks; an hour studying isn't an hour sleeping.

At the societal level there are two big ones:

- **Guns and butter**: defense versus consumer goods. Every resource in defense is a resource not spent on roads or scholarships.
- **Efficiency versus equality**: efficiency is how big the pie is, equality is how evenly it's sliced. Push harder on equality and you have to transfer resources from those who produced them — and the pie itself shrinks.

The instructor was careful here: saying the trade-off exists is **not an argument for doing nothing**. Every economy redistributes. The point is to be honest that the more you do, the less there is to divide.

## 5. Trade can make everyone better off: positive-sum, not zero-sum

Poker is zero-sum — every dollar I walk out with is a dollar someone else lost. Voluntary trade isn't. It's **positive-sum**; both sides gain.

The reductio is clean: if trading less is good, why not buy only domestic? And if that's good, why not only your state, only your city? The narrower your trading circle, the more you must produce yourself, and the less time you have for everything else.

Watch the wording: the principle says trade **can** make everyone better off, not that it **always** does. There are real losers, and that's the central question of the political economy of trade.

## 6. Markets — and where they stop

Principle six: markets are a good way to organize economic activity. Principle seven: governments can sometimes improve market outcomes. These two must be read as a pair.

One commonly repeated misconception gets an explicit correction: **"free market" does not mean sellers can do whatever they want, and it does not mean zero regulation.** It means sellers and buyers are free *within the law*. You cannot lie to customers about product quality. You cannot buy what's illegal.

"Free market = no rules" is simply wrong.

The contrast case is a planned economy: the state owns the means of production and decides what gets made. Principle six says markets win — but principle seven immediately adds: **when there's market failure**, government intervention can improve the outcome. The textbook case is an **externality** — someone's action imposes costs on others that the market price doesn't reflect.

One detail worth flagging: Mankiw's original wording is that markets are **usually** a good way to organize economic activity. The lecture says "best." Don't drop that "usually" — without it, principle seven has no ground to stand on.

## 7. The last three, in one line each

- **Living standards depend on productive ability**: a country's standard of living depends on its ability to produce what others want to buy. Same for a person — your future standard of living depends on your ability to produce something others will pay for. Which is why having a skill people will pay for matters, not how many hours you put in.
- **Print too much money and prices rise**: inflation is fundamentally a monetary phenomenon. Understand this and you understand why holding cash through inflation is a certain loss of purchasing power (which is just opportunity cost again).
- **Inflation and unemployment trade off in the short run**: pushing one down pushes the other up. The operative word is *short run* — the long-run Phillips curve is vertical; there's no stable trade-off.

## My own checklist

Compressed into five questions to run through before any decision:

1. **What's the next-best thing I'm giving up?** (Not how much money, not how much I'm down on paper.)
2. **Is the benefit of a little more greater than the cost of a little more?** (Marginal, not total.)
3. **Has the incentive structure changed — and whose behavior changes because of it?**
4. **Is there an external cost this price doesn't include?** (Might policy come along and price it in?)
5. **Is this zero-sum or positive-sum?** Is there an option that leaves both sides better off?

And five traps: treating sunk cost as cost; summing up every forgone option instead of taking the next-best one; comparing absolute magnitudes instead of relative ones; forecasting resources or demand with static division; and reading "free market" as no regulation — or, at the other extreme, reading "markets sometimes fail" as an argument for central planning.

One last note on context: the instructor's frame is American, and the free-market-versus-planned-economy binary has limited explanatory power for China. China is a mixed economy with strong industrial policy. The more useful question there is: **is the government pricing in an externality, or substituting its own judgment for the market's?**

What changed for me isn't what I know. It's what I ask. I used to ask "is this worth it." Now I ask "what am I giving up for it." The second question has an answer. The first one doesn't.
